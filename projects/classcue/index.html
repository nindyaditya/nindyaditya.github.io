One Teacher, 30 Students, and the Things You Miss During a Lesson

How could one teacher better understand what neurodivergent students, including those with ADHD, need during a lesson?

By Nindy Aditya Dewi

“You can start.”

Who do you check in with first?

Imagine a class of 30. The teacher has just worked through an example on the board. Now it’s the students’ turn to try it on their own.

Some start writing. One raises a hand. Others look back at the example. The teacher stops at one desk, while the rest of the class keeps working.

How can one teacher find out who needs a little more time, another explanation, or the steps written down?

It was clear on the board

While the teacher works through an example, the previous step is right there. Once it’s wiped away, students have to hold the steps in mind and use them on a new question.

Working memory helps us hold and use information in the moment. Kofler and colleagues found that working-memory difficulties varied across children with ADHD. Two students with ADHD can experience the same lesson differently. Kofler et al., 2019

The same classroom, different needs.

Neurodivergent students can experience the same lesson differently. A diagnosis gives some context. Hearing from the student helps the teacher understand what would help today.

These are illustrative examples. Needs vary between students and between lessons. Ask what helps.

I started with the learner. Then I looked around the classroom.

My early question was about cognitive accessibility in AI learning tools for neurodivergent students. Thinking about one teacher and around 30 students shifted my attention to how teachers could understand different needs during the same lesson.

I reviewed Gemini Guided Learning and Khanmigo student experiences, alongside teacher-facing planning tools. In the experiences and documentation reviewed, I found guided support and activity visibility. I wanted to explore a more complete exchange between student and teacher.

What would the design need to do?

Students needed a way to say what was difficult and what might help. Teachers needed enough context to decide what to do next. I used these needs to shape a check-in that continues after the teacher responds, so students can say what helped and what still feels unclear.

Mapping the exchange

The storyboard helped me trace what happens after a student speaks up. A check-in needs a teacher response, and that response needs a way back to the student.

View full-size blueprint ↗

Could you keep the steps on the board?

A short check-in at the end of the lesson asks how the pace felt, where students got stuck, and what would help. In this example, “Could you keep the steps on the board?” gives the teacher something concrete to try. Any student could ask without sharing a diagnosis.

Try student prototype ↗

What would the teacher see?

Reading up to 30 check-ins takes time. AI could group similar responses and suggest changes to the pace, which instructions to leave up, or which step to explain again. The teacher can open the original responses, decide what to try, and send a reply.

Try teacher prototype ↗

What if the student already has a support plan?

A teacher authorized to see the student’s Individualized Education Program (IEP) could read their check-in alongside the supports already confirmed in the plan. That gives the teacher a more specific place to follow up.

Try staff prototype ↗

“You can start.”

Next lesson, the teacher says it again. This time, the steps stay on the board. Afterward, students can tell the teacher whether that helped them get started and where they still needed support.

Could this fit into a real school day?

What I want to test

  • Can teachers review the groups and original responses in the time they have?
  • Do the groups preserve what students meant, including requests that only one student made?
  • Can students explain what helped after the teacher tries an adjustment?

I still need to test whether this is workable for teachers and helpful for students.
